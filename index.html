          The polar sketching toolkit (always applicable)

          1. Plot r-vs-θ first

          When you see r = f(θ), your first move is not to sketch in the polar plane — it is to plot r as a function of θ on standard xy axes. Mark the zeros, the maxes, and the mins. Then translate: at each θ where r = 0 the curve passes through the origin; at each θ where |r| peaks the curve reaches its furthest point in that direction. Connect with smooth arcs.

          Look up. The strip plot above is exactly that side graph, animating in lock-step with the polar trace. The orange dot in both views is at the same θ.

          2. Three symmetry tests

          • Polar axis (x-axis): replace θ → −θ. If the equation is unchanged, the graph is symmetric about the x-axis. (cos θ passes; sin θ fails.)
          • Line θ = π/2 (y-axis): replace θ → π − θ. (sin θ passes; cos θ fails.)
          • Origin: replace r → −r, or replace θ → θ + π. If unchanged, the graph is symmetric through the origin.

          Each passing test cuts your sketching work in half — you only sketch one side, then mirror.

          3. Period and range

          Don't trace redundant θ. cos kθ and sin kθ have period 2π/k, but the curve in the polar plane often closes earlier or later depending on whether negative-r values retrace existing branches. Quick rules:

          • Roses with even k repeat over [0, 2π]; with odd k the negative-r half retraces the positive-r half, so the curve closes over [0, π].
          • Limaçons / cardioids close over [0, 2π].
          • Spirals never close — pick the θ range you care about.

          4. What negative r means

          A point with negative r is plotted in the opposite direction — equivalent to (|r|, θ + π). That's why even-k roses get 2k petals (the negative-r half draws new petals between the positive ones) and why limaçons with a < b acquire an inner loop (the curve dips through the origin and comes out backwards).
